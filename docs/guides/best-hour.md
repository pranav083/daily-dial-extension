# Protect your best hour

Most people have an hour of the day when they are reliably sharpest. Fewer know which hour it is, and fewer still protect it — it tends to be the hour that other people's meetings land in.

The hour usually disappears into email, meetings, and quick syncs—not because you chose to spend it that way, but because nobody schedules work into your worst hour. The good hour is simply the one that's free on the calendar, so it gets filled.

Daily Dial can help you see the pattern. After a few weeks of logged days, an observation surfaces under History → "Worth noticing" that names your most productive hour and how often you actually protected it. You can also read the History heatmap and category trends yourself to spot when you're most focused. But once you see the pattern, the harder work begins: actually keeping it.

## Find it from your own data

Daily Dial tracks time in weighted categories across a 24-hour dial. After you've logged days for a few weeks, patterns emerge. The "Worth noticing" observation shows you your most productive hour—the time when you tend to make progress on difficult work, think clearly, or ship things.

This isn't a prescription. It's an observation from your data. A few days of logging won't show much; a few weeks will. If you prefer to read the heatmap yourself rather than wait for the observation, you can. Look for the hour that's consistently marked with high-value work, or the one where you get into flow. That's probably it.

## Why the best hour gets given away

The best hour is usually early: 8, 9, or 10 a.m. That's exactly when email lands, when standups are scheduled, when people send "quick sync" invites because they know you haven't started your day with meetings yet.

This isn't intentional. Nobody looks at the calendar and thinks, "I should book their worst hour." Instead, they book the hour that looks free—which is often free because it's the one you're usually most capable of protecting. Once you protect it, it stays protected. Until then, it fills.

## Take the time before someone else does

The most reliable way to keep the hour is a calendar block. Make it recurring. Make it visible to colleagues. Use whatever calendar system your workplace shares.

A standing block is more effective than reliance on willpower. It doesn't ask you to remember to say no; it makes it impossible for someone else to book the time in the first place. They see the block and move to a different hour. The system changes before you have to make a choice.

## Silence the hour

Muting a notification app is not the same as silencing it. You'll still see the banner, the badge, the sense that something is waiting. Close the application instead.

On macOS, set up a Focus mode for your best hour and enable it at the same time each day. Do the same on iOS if you carry your phone. On Android, use Digital Wellbeing to create a Focus mode. The menu paths vary by version and device, but the principle is the same: make it impossible to see the notification without deliberately opening the app.

Close mail and chat entirely rather than muting them. The distinction matters.

## Spend it on the hardest thing, not the easiest

The temptation is to "warm up" with admin, quick emails, or tidying. This spends your best hour on your cheapest work.

Easy work survives being done at a bad hour. You can answer email when you're tired. You can file things, process backlog, or handle routine tasks at 5 p.m. or on Friday afternoon and get decent results.

Hard work often does not. Writing something difficult, learning something new, or solving a problem that requires deep thinking is much more likely to succeed when your mind is sharp. Spend the hour on that. Spend the rest of the day on what survives distraction.

## Change where you are

Move to a different room, a different desk, a library, or a corner facing a wall instead of the open office. It costs nothing. Changing your physical context is often more effective than changing your tools or settings.

This works partly because you're away from the usual interruptions, and partly because the change itself signals to your brain that you're doing something different now. There's no scientific formula here—just try it and see what changes.

## When your best hour isn't available

Be honest about your constraints. If your most productive hour lands at 10 p.m., or you have caregiving responsibilities, or you work a fixed shift, "protect it" may be impossible.

Protecting an impossible hour is not practical advice. Instead, protect the best *available* hour. That might be 30 minutes instead of a full hour. It might be a different hour than the one the data suggests. It might be Saturday morning. Work with what you can actually control.

This advice assumes a degree of control over your calendar that not everyone has. If you don't have that control, the same principles apply to whatever hour you can genuinely protect: block it, silence it, spend it on hard things, and change where you are if you can. The goal is the same. The route is just different.
